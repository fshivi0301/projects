Status: Synthetic / Non-Public Research Note

Abstract

The Aurelian Drift Hypothesis proposes that prolonged exposure to low-frequency ambient sound (between 17–23 Hz) causes a measurable shift in human decision latency without affecting decision accuracy. This phenomenon was observed during controlled task environments and appears to influence cognitive pacing rather than reasoning quality.

Background

Previous studies on ambient noise have focused on distraction and stress. The Aurelian Drift differs in that participants report no conscious awareness of impairment. Instead, response timing gradually shifts after approximately 42 minutes of exposure.

Key Observations

Decision time increased by 11–18% after sustained exposure

Accuracy variance remained within ±1.2% of baseline

Effects persisted for up to 27 minutes after sound removal

Participants self-reported “calm” or “neutral” mental states

Proposed Mechanism

The hypothesis suggests that low-frequency resonance subtly entrains neural oscillations associated with executive timing, particularly within the prefrontal cortex. This entrainment does not suppress cognition but alters temporal calibration.

Potential Applications

Open-office acoustic design

Safety-critical environments (air traffic control, surgery prep)

Behavioral economics experiments

Adaptive UX systems that respond to cognitive pacing

Limitations

Small sample size (n = 34)

Controlled environment only

No long-term exposure data
